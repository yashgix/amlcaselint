CONSENT ORDER IMPOSING CIVIL MONEY PENALTY
The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) has conducted a civil enforcement
investigation and determined that grounds exist to impose a Civil Money Penalty against Bank of
America, N.A. (Bank of America or the Bank) for violations of the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) and
its implementing regulations.1 Bank of America admits to the Statement of Facts and Violations
set forth below, consents to the issuance of this Consent Order, agrees to pay the civil money
penalty imposed in this Consent Order, and agrees to comply with the provisions of this Consent
Order, including, but not limited to, the Undertakings.

D. Bank of America Failed to Implement and Maintain an AML Program
Bank of America willfully failed to implement and maintain an AML program that met
several of the BSA’s requirements during the Relevant Time Period. The Bank’s failures, described
below, related to several regulatory requirements (commonly referred to as “pillars”) of the AML
program.
The Bank willfully failed to implement and maintain adequate internal controls for BSA
compliance, primarily due to critical deficiencies and gaps in its proprietary transaction monitoring
system, which resulted in both missed and delayed detection and reporting by Bank of America of
over $5 billion of suspicious activity, with roughly $1 billion of this activity implicating national
security-related and other priority illicit finance risks, including suspicious transactions
indicative of Chinese Money Laundering Networks (CMLNs), fraud, and potential trade-based
money laundering (TBML) with a substantial nexus to the southwest border. Further, Bank of
America’s AML program deficiencies extended to its CDD procedures, including for high-risk
customers that operated in the online auto auction industry, further contributing to such unreported
or untimely reported suspicious activity.

1. Inadequate Internal Controls
Throughout the Relevant Time Period, Bank of America willfully failed to implement and
maintain a system of internal controls sufficient to assure the Bank’s ongoing compliance with the
BSA and its implementing regulations. This resulted from several issues related to the design and
implementation of Bank of America’s proprietary transaction monitoring system, Event Processor
(EP), the primary control that the Bank used to fulfill its obligation to identify and report suspicious
activity. As further detailed in Section II.F, infra, these issues meant the Bank did not report large
volumes of suspicious activity implicating national security-related and other priority illicit finance
risks, including over $600 million of transactions with indicia of potential TBML involving
salvaged vehicles (roughly one-third of which had a nexus to the southwest border) and hundreds
of millions of dollars of transactions consistent with a CMLN typology.
The issues with EP resulted in the Bank relying upon law enforcement, other financial
institutions, and internal manual referral processes to identify and report suspicious activity: for
multiple years within the Relevant Time Period, more than one-third of Bank of America’s
suspicious activity investigations were prompted by such events17 instead of the Bank’s own
automated, internal controls, and as described below, such reliance appears to have occurred at

Background on Event Processor. In 2010, Bank of America designed and, through a
“proof of concept,” first implemented EP to manage the Bank’s transaction monitoring alerts of
potentially suspicious activity. Bank of America designed EP to act as an interconnected model
system, the main objectives of which were the monitoring, identifying, investigating, and reporting
of suspicious activity. The Bank’s records described EP as the first of its kind in the industry, with
“very little documentation or literature to draw from in its creation.” EP was created to solve
challenges associated with reviewing alerts associated with individual transactions, including that
“there were not enough investigators to work all of the alerts that were being generated by
detection channels.”19

As designed, EP resulted in individual alerts of potentially suspicious activity associated
with a transaction (referred to by the Bank as “events”) generally not being subject to manual
review by an investigator. Instead (and as depicted in the graphic below),20 EP individually
assigned a weighted score to each alert by assigning the alert “risk factors” and consolidated—
generally at the customer level—the alerts generated within a defined time period (described
below) into groups (Event Groups). Event Groups consisted of all individual alerts associated with
a customer within a defined time period (described below), with the weighted risk score assigned
to these alerts combined to generate a risk score for the Event Group as a whole. Absent autopromotion (as described below), an Event Group generally would not be subject to investigation
until its combined risk score reached a threshold score of 20 points. This threshold, “initially set
by stakeholder judgment,” remained in place until December 2023.

10

During the Relevant Time Period, EP included hundreds of risk factors that ranged in
weights from 0 to 20 points. Risk factors were configured to apply at the event group, event, party,
and account levels and were processed two times each weekday. EP’s inclusion of “Event Group
level” risk factors meant that such risk factors contributed only once to the Event Group’s total
score, irrespective of how many times the associated alert had been triggered. For example, one
of the alerts in effect during the Relevant Time Period involved certain large aggregated incoming
wires for the relevant customer that was assigned a weight of 1 point within EP, but because EP
calculated this as an Event Group level score, no matter how many times this alert generated, it
could only ever contribute 1 point to the relevant Event Group.
By contrast, many risk factors were scored at the “event level,” meaning their score counted
towards the Event Group score each time the risk factor was triggered. The Bank’s documentation
on EP noted that this summation of scores within an Event Group was selected “as the simplest
way to combine weights for disparate detection channels,” but also acknowledged that “detection
channels could interact in a non-linear fashion.” In other words, because EP’s design allowed for
a large amount of potentially suspicious activity to accumulate within an Event Group before it
would be subject to investigation, various different types of potentially suspicious activity could
combine within the Event Group and result in that Event Group accumulating risk at a much faster
rate than the risk weights of its individual component alerts—even if properly calibrated on an
individual basis—would suggest.
In order for a case to be created and thereby trigger review by a Bank investigator, the
Event Group must have reached the threshold score of 20 points within 13 months of the oldest
active alert within the Event Group, with any alerts (other than those that formed an Event Group
already subject to investigation) older than 13 months subject to “retirement,” such that they would

11

still be included in the Event Group, but no longer contribute to that Event Group’s total score.
The Event Group, however, would continue to generate new alerts so long as it contained at least
one alert that was less than 13 months old.
The 13-month window was chosen based on “stakeholder judgment” to “ensure that all
activity within a full calendar year would be considered,” as Bank of America believed that a larger
window “increases the probability that a few false positive[s] . . . might combine to reach the case
[creation] threshold.” However, as detailed below, many risk weights were not appropriately
calibrated to reflect the relevant risks; as a result, many cases that would have resulted in SARs
never reached the 20-point threshold within this timeframe.
Once an Event Group received a score of 20 points, it formed a “case” that was sent to an
AML investigator for manual review. One of the ways that an Event Group could reach this
threshold was through what the Bank deemed to be “auto-promote” events that automatically
caused an Event Group to form a case subject to review by an investigator, a material portion of
which were based on “external events” occurring outside EP, such as the Bank’s receipt of seizure
warrants or a subpoena. As noted above, FinCEN’s investigation found that, for several years
within the Relevant Time Period, this “auto promotion” feature of EP contributed to more than
one-third of all investigations that the Bank conducted in a given year.
The Bank performed regular evaluations of EP to determine if EP identified all activity that
it should have been identifying and ensure that EP was prioritizing alerted activity in accordance
with its attendant risk. However, as detailed below, the testing was inadequate because, among
other things, it did not focus on whether EP was appropriately capturing Bank of America’s risk,
and even where such testing did highlight issues with EP, the Bank failed to reasonably address
such issues.

a) Risk Weights Not Properly Calibrated for Attendant Risks
As detailed below, many of EP’s risk factor weights were largely unsubstantiated and were
not sufficiently anchored in relevant risks. An internal “Performance Analysis” report on EP issued
in April 2019, described, among other aspects of EP, a risk factor for the ATM Large Cash Deposit
Model that required five conditions to trigger an alert. The report found that the necessity to fulfill
all five conditions was a “very strict requirement” and that a “study of SAR case narratives from
2016 to 2018 found that SARs could be filed even when only one condition…was satisfied. In
other words, it might not be necessary to require all five conditions to be satisfied at the same
time.” Moreover, the report observed that this risk factor was weighted only one point in EP, but
the Bank’s own testing had found that this risk factor had a SAR yield of 56.75%.24

b) Risk Factors Not Sufficiently Calibrated, Tested, or Updated Over
Time
Risk factor weights in EP remained static over much of the Relevant Time Period, further
contributing to a disconnect between EP and the Bank’s risk profile. From 2018 to 2023, 76% of
all risk factor weights remained the same, while the relatively few risk factor weights that
changed tended to result in weight reductions: of the roughly 25% of risk factor weights that
experienced a change within this timeframe, approximately 80% of such changes were
decreases to the risk factor weight. Even when Bank of America was aware that risk factors
needed increased weights, the Bank was delayed in recalibrating and changing these risk factors.
Some of these delays were also attributed to the ineffectiveness of the Bank’s internal testing and
recommendations.
For example, as mentioned above, many cash-related risk factors had weights of one point
but SAR yields above 50%. For the model “ATM Large Cash Deposit,” a 2019 report found that
16

“the current weights do not align with the high SAR yield.” The report acknowledged that Bank
of America had added four new risk factors to this model during the most recent model
enhancement, but the report also found that “all four risk factors have high SAR yields, but each
of them only carries a risk weight of 1-pt.” The weights associated with these four types of alerts
remained unchanged through 2024.
Additionally, the April 2020 report found that certain types of alerts in a cash structuring
model with risk factor weights of only 1 or 2 points had high SAR yields, even after enhancement
of the model. The report’s accompanying recommendation about these risk factors described the
model’s performance as having “improved significantly” and recommended that the Bank monitor
the performance of one type of alert generated by this model that did not have a high SAR yield.
This report did not include a recommendation to address the low risk factor weights for the many
other types of alerts generated by this model that were found to have had high SAR yields.
In late 2021, and in response to certain of the recommendations in these reports, Bank of
America published a report summarizing a “Risk Factor Calibration Model” for the weights of risk
factors within EP. However, this work was informed by the Bank’s continued focus on improving
SAR yield without proper consideration of the risks involved.

E. Due Diligence Programs for Correspondent Accounts for Foreign Financial

Foreign correspondent accounts are gateways to the U.S. financial system that can present
heightened AML/CTF risks depending on the unique facts and circumstances of the account.46
Bank of America failed to establish a due diligence program that, with respect to correspondent
accounts that the Bank maintained for its own foreign branches and affiliates, satisfied regulatory
requirements. Until late 2024, the Bank had a policy permitting “[s]implified” due diligence that
imposed “reduced requirements” for the correspondent accounts of the Bank’s foreign branches
and affiliates.
FinCEN’s investigation of this issue, which focused on twelve of the Bank’s foreign
branches and affiliates, found that the Bank’s “simplified” due diligence caused files for the
relevant foreign branches and affiliates to (i) lack sufficient information about the anticipated
activity in the correspondent account and the account’s purpose; (ii) contain general statements
about the nature of the foreign branch or affiliate’s business and markets served, such as “treasury
services” or “cash management;” and (iii) have insufficient information about the supervisory
regime applicable to the foreign branch or affiliate, such as merely providing the name of the local
regulatory body. These due diligence files often consisted of information irrelevant to the Bank’s
ability to detect and report, on an ongoing basis, suspicious activity conducted through or involving
the correspondent accounts (e.g., graphs depicting the price of the Bank’s parent holding
company’s publicly traded stock).

information—foreign branch risk assessments and industry questionnaires—such materials did not

implicating national security-related and other priority illicit finance risks, including suspicious

critical financial intelligence.

VIOLATIONS
FinCEN has determined that Bank of America willfully violated the BSA and its

implementing regulations during the Relevant Time Period. Specifically, FinCEN has determined
that the Bank willfully failed to implement and maintain an AML program that met the minimum
requirements of the BSA, in violation of 31 U.S.C. § 5318 (h)(1) and 31 C.F.R.
§ 1020.210(a). Additionally, FinCEN determined that Bank of America willfully failed to conduct
due diligence on correspondent accounts in the United States for foreign financial institutions, in
violation of 31 U.S.C. § 5318(i) and 31 C.F.R. § 1010.610. FinCEN also determined that the Bank
willfully failed to accurately and timely report suspicious transactions to FinCEN, in violation of
31 U.S.C. § 5318(g) and 31 C.F.R. § 1020.320.
IV.

ENFORCEMENT FACTORS

As summarized below, FinCEN considered all factors outlined in the Statement on
Enforcement of the Bank Secrecy Act issued August 18, 2020, when deciding whether to impose
a civil money penalty in this matter.54
1.

Nature and seriousness of the violations, including extent of possible harm to the
public and the amounts involved: Bank of America’s violations presented significant risk
of serious harm to the U.S. financial system. During the Relevant Time Period the Bank’s
primary internal control for identifying and reporting suspicious transactions was not
reasonably designed, including by allowing an increasing amount of suspicious activity to
remain uninvestigated sufficiently or to be investigated only after an extended period of
time (often, more than a year). Bank of America’s own internal testing identified these

54

FinCEN, Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) Statement on Enforcement of the Bank Secrecy Act
(Aug. 18, 2020).

60

issues, yet the Bank failed to timely and adequately respond to them. As indicated in the
examples in Section II.D.3, supra, the Bank failed to develop accurate risk profiles and to
conduct risk-based ongoing monitoring for customers engaged in tens of billions of dollars’
worth of transactions that Bank personnel had identified as such a serious concern that one
customer engaged in this activity was initially recommended to be offboarded. These
failures resulted in the Bank failing to timely report over $5 billion in suspicious
transactions, including more than $1 billion of suspicious transactions that implicated
significant U.S. national security-related and other priority illicit finance risks, such as
suspicious transactions with indicia of CMLNs, fraud, and potential TBML with a nexus
to the southwest border. This failure deprived law enforcement of timely and critical
financial information pertaining to suspicious activity.
2.

Impact or harm of the violations on FinCEN’s mission to safeguard the financial
system from illicit use, combat money laundering, and promote national security: As
a general matter, SARs represent one of the most important tools to FinCEN and law
enforcement in fighting financial crime, both in proactively identifying potential illicit
activity and in understanding the scope and scale of that illicit activity. FinCEN and law
enforcement must be able to rely on financial institutions to remain vigilant and comply
with their obligation to report suspicious activity, and Bank of America’s violations caused
a significant gap in the timely identification and reporting of suspicious activity. Bank of
America’s flawed approach to suspicious activity monitoring resulted in the Bank relying
upon law enforcement inquiries and other manual processes to alert the Bank to potentially
suspicious activity, with more than one-third of the Bank’s investigations driven by
“promotion” events—much of which consisted of outreach from law enforcement and

61

external stakeholders—rather than its own internal controls. Although a bank’s reasonable
cooperation with law enforcement inquiries, participation in Section 314(b) information
sharing, and employee referral processes remain important, the nature and extent of Bank
of America’s reliance on those sources to alert it to relevant risks resulted in the Bank
contravening two of the BSA’s key purposes: 55 the Bank’s failures materially increased the
risks that money may have been laundered through the Bank and reduced the timely
reporting of highly useful information to law enforcement. Critically, and as noted above,
much of the suspicious activity that Bank of America failed to timely report implicated
national security-related and other priority illicit finance risks, including suspicious activity
indicative of CMLNs, fraud, and potential TBML with a nexus to the southwest border.
Finally, the Bank’s flawed approach to suspicious activity reporting also undermined
FinCEN’s ability to receive timely financial intelligence for analytical and investigative
purposes, including its ability to disseminate such information to other financial
institutions. FinCEN afforded greater weight to this factor given the nature and extent of
the impact, including the potential implications for priority illicit finance risks.
3.

Pervasiveness of wrongdoing within an entity, including management’s complicity in,
condoning or enabling of, or knowledge of the conduct underlying the violations:
During the Relevant Time Period, Bank of America had numerous opportunities to correct
the deficiencies identified by FinCEN’s investigation, including from findings of its own
internal testing. Notably, given the Bank’s enterprise-wide application of EP and its
reliance on EP as the primary control for identifying potentially suspicious activity, the
associated deficiencies (and the compliance expenditures saved from meaningfully
